### 261 · Spinning jenny (`spinning_jenny`)

The spinning jenny let one worker spin several threads at once. A hand-turned wheel drove a row of spindles while a sliding clamp drew out the cotton, imitating a spinner's hand. James Hargreaves of Lancashire built it around 1764 and patented it in 1770. Early machines carried eight spindles; jenny yarn was soft, suited mainly to weft.

### 262 · Water frame (`water_frame`)

The water frame spun strong cotton thread by drawing fibre between pairs of rollers turning at rising speeds, then twisting it onto a bobbin with a flyer. Richard Arkwright patented it in 1769, adapting roller drafting tried by Lewis Paul and John Wyatt in 1738. Its firm yarn suited warp. Arkwright's water-powered mill at Cromford ran from 1771 in shifts.

### 263 · Spinning mule (`spinning_mule`)

The spinning mule, completed by Samuel Crompton near Bolton around 1779, joined the jenny's moving carriage to the water frame's drafting rollers, hence its hybrid name. Rollers drew the cotton out; the carriage then ran back, stretching and twisting it further. The thread was fine and strong enough for muslins, long imported mainly from India. Crompton never patented it.

### 264 · Power loom (`power_loom`)

A power loom performs weaving's repeated motions by machine: raising warp threads, throwing the shuttle across, and beating in the weft. It mechanised John Kay's flying shuttle (1733). Edmund Cartwright patented early designs from 1785, but practical looms needed later improvements by others. By about 1820 steam looms were spreading in Britain; Waltham, Massachusetts, wove by water power from 1814.

### 265 · Saw gin (`cotton_gin_saw`)

A saw gin separates cotton fibre from its sticky seeds: toothed discs on a turning cylinder pull lint through slots too narrow for seeds. Eli Whitney patented a wire-toothed gin in 1793; Hodgen Holmes's 1796 patent used circular saws. Cheap ginning made short-staple upland cotton profitable across the American South, where the resulting boom greatly expanded plantation slavery.

### 266 · Jacquard loom (`jacquard`)

A Jacquard head selects which warp threads rise for each weft pass from a chain of punched cards: where a card has a hole, the linked hook lifts its thread. It automated the drawloom's figure harness, worked by an assistant, the drawboy. Joseph-Marie Jacquard developed it in Lyon about 1804, building on earlier devices by Bouchon (1725), Falcon (1728) and Vaucanson (1745).

### 280 · Reinforced concrete (`reinforced_concrete`)

Reinforced concrete embeds iron or steel bars in concrete: the concrete resists crushing, the metal carries tension, and both expand similarly with heat. Joseph Monier, a Paris gardener, patented reinforced planters in 1867 and later wider uses; Joseph-Louis Lambot and William Wilkinson had experimented earlier. François Hennebique's 1892 system joined beams and columns into monolithic frames for buildings and bridges.

### 339 · Steel-frame skyscraper (`skyscraper`)

A steel-frame skyscraper carries floors and outer walls on a metal skeleton of columns and beams instead of thick masonry, so height no longer requires ever-thicker base walls. Chicago's Home Insurance Building, designed by William Le Baron Jenney and completed in 1885, is often called the first, though this is debated. Cheap steel, fireproofing and passenger elevators made such towers usable.

### 342 · Numerical control and robotics (`numerical_control`)

Numerical control directs a machine tool by coded instructions instead of a machinist's hands. John Parsons proposed the idea for cutting aircraft parts, and MIT's Servomechanisms Laboratory demonstrated a tape-controlled milling machine in 1952. In 1961 the Unimate, a programmable arm designed by George Devol and promoted by Joseph Engelberger, began handling hot die-castings at a General Motors plant.

### 393 · Desalination (`desalination`)

Desalination removes dissolved salt from seawater or brackish water. Ships and chemists distilled small amounts for centuries, but large plants came after the Second World War. Multi-stage flash plants, boiling heated brine in chambers of falling pressure, spread from about 1960, often beside Gulf power stations. Reverse osmosis forces water through a membrane; practical membranes emerged at UCLA around 1960.

### 454 · Sanitary engineering (sewerage and filtered water) (`sanitary_engineering`)
