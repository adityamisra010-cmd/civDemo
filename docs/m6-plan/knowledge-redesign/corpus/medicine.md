### 79 · Trepanation (`trepanation`)

Trepanation is the deliberate removal of a piece of the skull vault by scraping, grooving or drilling, first with stone tools. Healed bone edges show that many patients survived. A burial at Ensisheim, Alsace, dated about 5100-4900 BCE, is among the earliest widely accepted cases, and the practice arose independently elsewhere, notably in the pre-Columbian Andes.

### 108 · Medical texts (`medicine_recorded`)

Medical texts fixed treatments in writing, so remedies could be copied and taught beyond one healer's memory. A Sumerian clay tablet from Nippur, of about 2100 BCE, lists prescriptions. ### 240 · Variolation (`inoculation`)

Variolation protected against smallpox by deliberately infecting a healthy person with material from a smallpox sore or scab, usually producing a milder illness and lasting immunity. It was practised in China, where powdered scabs were blown into the nose, and in India, the Ottoman world and West Africa before reaching Britain in 1721. Recipients could still die or infect others.

### 241 · Systematic anatomy (`anatomy_dissection`)

### 293 · Vaccination (`vaccination`)

Vaccination replaced smallpox material with cowpox, a related cattle disease, giving protection without variolation's risk of severe smallpox. Edward Jenner tested it in England in 1796, drawing on the rural belief that milkmaids who caught cowpox escaped smallpox; the farmer Benjamin Jesty had tried it in 1774. Vaccination spread worldwide within decades. Smallpox eradication was certified in 1980.

### 294 · Surgical anaesthesia (`anaesthesia`)

Surgical anaesthesia makes a patient insensible to pain for the length of an operation. In Japan, Hanaoka Seishū had operated under an herbal general anaesthetic in 1804. Painless surgery permitted slower, more deliberate operations.

### 295 · Germ theory (`germ_theory`)

Germ theory holds that specific microorganisms cause specific infectious diseases. Louis Pasteur linked microbes to fermentation and spoilage, and Robert Koch tied anthrax (1876) and then tuberculosis (1882) to identified bacteria using culture and staining.

### 296 · Antiseptic and aseptic surgery (`antisepsis`)

Antiseptic surgery applied germ theory to wounds. In Glasgow from 1867, Joseph Lister treated dressings, instruments and incisions with carbolic acid and reported fewer infections after compound fractures. Aseptic practice later aimed to keep germs out entirely, with sterilised instruments, gowns and gloves. Together with anaesthesia, it made abdominal and other long operations survivable far more often.

### 324 · Penicillin (`antibiotic_penicillin`)

### 326 · Antibiotic resistance management (`antibiotic_resistance`)

Managing resistance relies on surveillance, prudent prescribing and new drugs.

### 336 · X-ray imaging (`xray`)

X-rays are invisible, penetrating radiation that Wilhelm Röntgen discovered on 8 November 1895 while experimenting with a cathode-ray tube in Würzburg. Early operators, unaware of the hazard, suffered serious radiation injuries.

### 384 · Monoclonal antibodies (`monoclonal_antibodies`)

### 438 · Physiology (`physiology`)

Physiology studies how the body's parts work, not only what they look like. William Harvey's De motu cordis (1628) used ligature experiments and estimates of how much blood the heart pumps to show that blood circulates continuously. Ibn al-Nafis had described the blood's passage through the lungs in thirteenth-century Cairo. Capillaries, which closed Harvey's circuit, were first seen in 1661.

### 446 · Epidemiology (`epidemiology`)

Epidemiology studies how disease is distributed across populations and why. In 1662 John Graunt analysed London's bills of mortality, counting deaths by cause. In 1854 John Snow mapped cholera deaths around a Soho water pump and compared water companies' customers, arguing that cholera spread through water. Randomised trials, such as Britain's 1948 streptomycin trial, extended its comparative method.

### 475 · Sulfonamides (`sulfonamides`)

Researchers in Paris then showed that the active part was sulfanilamide. Deaths from childbed fever fell from the mid-1930s.
