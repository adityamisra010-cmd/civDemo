### 2 · Oldowan flaking (`knapping_oldowan`)

Knapping strikes one stone against another to detach sharp-edged flakes. Oldowan tools, named after Olduvai Gorge, Tanzania, appear at Gona, Ethiopia, by about 2.6 million years ago. At Nyayanga, Kenya, they lie among cut-marked hippopotamus bones, with wear from processing animals and plants. Cruder Lomekwian pieces from Kenya, about 3.3 million years old, remain debated.

### 3 · Prepared-core (Levallois) (`knapping_levallois`)

In the Levallois method a knapper first shapes a stone core so that one blow detaches a flake of planned size and outline. The approach grew from simpler prepared-core methods and became widespread in Africa and western Eurasia around 300,000 years ago; in southwestern China it appears between about 170,000 and 80,000 years ago.

### 4 · Blade production (`knapping_blade`)

Blades are long, narrow, parallel-sided flakes struck in series from a carefully maintained core. Blade and bladelet production from prismatic cores is documented in southern Africa's Howiesons Poort, roughly 65,000 years ago, and blade industries spread across Eurasia from about 45,000 years ago, as at Bacho Kiro, Bulgaria. One core could supply blanks for many tool forms.

### 5 · Pressure flaking (`pressure_flaking`)

Instead of striking, a knapper presses a pointed tool of bone, antler or wood against an edge to push off thin, controlled flakes. At Blombos Cave, South Africa, at least half the Still Bay points, made from about 76,000 years ago in heat-treated silcrete, were finished this way. Solutrean knappers in France and Spain later used pressure retouch too.

### 6 · Heat treatment of stone (`heat_treatment_stone`)

Controlled heating of silcrete or chert alters the stone so that it fractures more smoothly; heated pieces leave glossy rather than dull flake scars. Evidence from Pinnacle Point, South Africa, points to the practice by about 164,000 years ago, and experiments showed unheated Blombos silcrete could hardly be pressure-flaked. It is an early case of fire altering a material.

### 7 · Microliths and composite tools (`microlith`)

Microliths are small segments of blades or bladelets, often blunted along one edge, set in rows or at angles into wooden or bone shafts. Much later, Mesolithic Europeans used similar inserts as replaceable barbs and cutting edges.

### 8 · Ground-edge axe (`ground_stone_early`)

### 10 · Birch-bark tar (`birch_tar`)

A tar-coated Neanderthal flint recovered from the Zandmotor off the Dutch coast is about 50,000 years old.

### 48 · Updraft kiln (`kiln_updraft`)

An updraft kiln separates fire from pots: fuel burns in a lower chamber and hot gases rise through a perforated floor into the ware above. Enclosure allows higher, steadier temperatures and some control of the atmosphere. In northern Mesopotamia simple pit kilns appear in the early sixth millennium BCE and two-chamber kilns by its middle, in the Halaf period.

### 63 · Native copper working (`copper_native`)

Some copper occurs naturally as metal. Hammering it cold makes it harder but brittle, and heating (annealing) restores workability, so beads, pins and awls could be made without smelting. Annealed native copper appears at Cayonu in southeastern Anatolia around the end of the ninth millennium BCE, and independently near North America's Great Lakes from about 8,500 years ago.

### 64 · Copper smelting (`copper_smelting`)

Smelting reduces copper minerals, such as green malachite, to metal by heating them with charcoal in a hot, oxygen-poor fire. Slag from Belovode, Serbia, about 5000 BCE, is among the earliest securely dated evidence, though comparable Near Eastern finds may prove as old. Smelting freed metalworkers from scarce native copper and made metal a regular trade good.

### 80 · Arsenical bronze (`arsenical_bronze`)

Copper containing a few percent arsenic, often from smelting arsenic-rich ores, is harder than pure copper and casts well, without any tin. Its fumes endangered metalworkers.

### 81 · Tin bronze (`tin_bronze`)

Alloying copper with up to about ten percent tin makes tin bronze. Tin bronzes appear in Mesopotamia and Anatolia in the early third millennium BCE, although neither region had significant tin sources; a bronze foil from Plocnik, Serbia, about 4650 BCE, is claimed as earliest. Bronze-making there therefore depended on tin brought from elsewhere.

### 83 · Lost-wax casting (`lost_wax`)

### 103 · Glaze and faience (`glass_glaze`)

Glaze could be brushed on, or form from a glazing powder in which an object was buried.

### 104 · Core-formed glass (`glass_core`)
