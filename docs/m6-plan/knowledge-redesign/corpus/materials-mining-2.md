### 238 · Coke smelting (`coke`)

Coke is coal baked with little air to drive off its volatile matter, leaving a strong, porous carbon fuel. In 1709 Abraham Darby fired his Coalbrookdale blast furnace in Shropshire with coke as charcoal grew costly. Earlier trials had faltered; his was Europe's first lasting success, permitting larger furnaces and much cheaper cast iron.

### 246 · Lead crystal (`glass_lead`)

Lead crystal is glass made heavy and brilliant with lead oxide. In 1674 George Ravenscroft patented a clear English glass meant to rival rock crystal; when early pieces clouded, a defect called crizzling, he added lead, a change dated between 1674 and 1676. The denser, more refractive glass suited cut tableware and, later, the flint elements of achromatic lenses.

### 252 · Puddling and rolling (`puddling`)

Puddling turned brittle pig iron into tough wrought iron without charcoal. In a coal-fired reverberatory furnace, where flame rather than fuel touched the metal, workers stirred molten iron until air burned out its carbon; the iron was then shingled and passed through grooved rolls. Henry Cort patented the rolls in 1783 and puddling in 1784; Welsh ironmasters made them practical.

### 253 · Hot blast (`hot_blast`)

Hot blast means preheating the air blown into a blast furnace. James Beaumont Neilson, manager of the Glasgow gas works, patented the idea in 1828. Heating the air to about 300 degrees Fahrenheit cut coal per ton of iron by over a third in one reported case, allowed raw coal instead of coke, and made Scotland's blackband ironstone worth smelting.

### 254 · Bessemer converter (`bessemer`)

The Bessemer process made steel by blowing air through molten pig iron in a tilting vessel; burning out carbon and silicon released enough heat to keep the charge liquid without added fuel. Henry Bessemer described it at Cheltenham in August 1856, while William Kelly reached a similar idea independently in the United States. It made bulk steel far cheaper.

### 255 · Open-hearth steel (`open_hearth`)

Open-hearth steelmaking melted pig iron with scrap and ore in a shallow hearth heated by Carl Wilhelm Siemens's regenerative furnace, which used waste heat to preheat gas and air. Pierre-Émile Martin first made steel this way at Sireuil, France, in 1864-65. Slower than a converter, it let steelmakers sample and adjust each charge, and it consumed scrap.

### 256 · Basic (Gilchrist-Thomas) process (`basic_process`)

Acid-lined converters could not remove phosphorus, which made steel brittle. Sidney Gilchrist Thomas and his cousin Percy Gilchrist, experimenting at Blaenavon in Wales, lined the converter with basic material such as lime, which drew phosphorus into the slag. Announced in 1878, the method opened phosphoric ores in Lorraine and elsewhere, and its slag was sold as fertiliser.

### 279 · Portland cement (`portland_cement`)

Portland cement is made by burning limestone or chalk with clay into clinker and grinding it to a powder that hardens with water. Joseph Aspdin patented the name in 1824, after Portland stone, but his product was probably underburned. Firing at much higher temperatures, as Isaac Charles Johnson did around 1845-50, produced the stronger modern material.

### 306 · Aluminium by electrolysis (`aluminium`)

Aluminium is abundant in rocks but bound tightly to oxygen, so the metal was long a costly rarity. In 1886 Charles Martin Hall in the United States and Paul Héroult in France independently found that alumina dissolved in molten cryolite could be split by electric current. With Karl Josef Bayer's cheaper alumina from bauxite (1888-89), prices fell steeply.

### 307 · Superalloys (`superalloy`)

Superalloys are nickel-based alloys that keep their strength when red-hot and under stress. Patents from 1929 showed that titanium and aluminium could form tiny strengthening particles in nickel-chromium. After a British government request in 1939, Mond Nickel tested over a thousand experimental alloys; Nimonic 80, introduced in 1941, became the standard turbine-blade alloy of British gas turbines.

### 353 · Composite materials (`composites`)

Composite materials embed strong fibres in a resin so that each covers the other's weakness. A US patent for making glass fibres with air jets was sought in 1933, and glass fibre was paired with new plastic resins from 1936. In 1963 Britain's Royal Aircraft Establishment developed strong carbon fibre, later used in airframes and turbine blades.

### 408 · Nanoscale engineering (`nanotechnology`)

Nanoscale engineering works with structures roughly 1 to 100 nanometres across, where surface and quantum effects change how materials behave. The scanning tunnelling microscope, built at IBM Zurich in 1981, made single atoms visible; in 1989 IBM researchers moved xenon atoms to spell the company's initials. Fullerenes (1985) and carbon nanotubes (1991) followed as new carbon forms.

### 413 · Asteroid mining (`asteroid_mining`)

Asteroid mining is the proposed extraction of water, metals or other materials from asteroids, for use in space or on Earth. It has not yet been done. Robotic missions have returned only small samples, such as Japan's Hayabusa2 from asteroid Ryugu in 2020. Two firms founded to pursue it, Planetary Resources and Deep Space Industries, were sold in 2018-19.

### 417 · Materials science (`frontier_materials`)

Materials science studies how a material's structure, from atoms to grains, governs its properties, and how processing changes that structure. It took shape as a discipline in the 1950s, when Northwestern University broadened metallurgy into a department renamed materials science in 1958-59. Recent programmes, such as the US Materials Genome Initiative (2011), pair computation with experiment.

### 452 · Scientific metallurgy (metallography) (`metallography`)

Metallography studies the inner structure of metals. From 1863 to 1865 Henry Clifton Sorby of Sheffield polished and etched steel and examined it under reflected-light microscopes, revealing distinct constituents. By about 1900 Roberts-Austen and Bakhuis Roozeboom had drawn the iron-carbon diagram, explaining hardening and tempering that smiths had long practised without knowing why.
